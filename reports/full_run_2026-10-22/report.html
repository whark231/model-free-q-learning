Summary

TL;DR / In plain terms

Both runs worked: no crashes, and two independent reviews re-derived the results and this report's numbers from the training logs. On both grids the agent learns to hold SPY and GLD, often with one bond slice (usually IEF), which is the right neighbourhood. It picks the exact best portfolio on its grid in 3 of 10 seeds on the 20% grid and 5 of 10 on the 25% grid.

The coarser 25% grid gives the better final result at this training budget. Its best portfolio sits further from the true optimum, but the agent finds it far more reliably, so its median total shortfall is smaller, 14.3 bps/yr against 19.8 bps/yr.

Key concepts to understand

  • Certainty-equivalent (CE) loss, in bps per year. Every portfolio has a score U = expected return minus (γ/2) × variance. The difference in U between two portfolios, times 252 days and 10,000, is how much guaranteed annual return the investor would give up by holding the worse one. 25 bps/yr means a quarter of a percent per year.
  • Discretization gap vs. learning gap. The grid cannot hold the exact optimum, so even a perfect learner loses the discretization gap. The learning gap is what the agent loses on top of that by not picking the grid's best portfolio. Their sum is the total gap to the continuous optimum.
  • Greedy action. The portfolio the agent rates highest right now. Most of the time-series charts track it, because it is what the agent would hold if it stopped exploring.
  • Seed. One full training run with its own random draws. Ten seeds per grid show how much the outcome depends on luck.

Key takeaways

  • The agent settles on its final portfolio early: by 47 million simulated days (median) on the 20% grid and 18.5 million on the 25% grid, out of 260 million. Apart from brief excursions (none longer than 434 episodes), the second half of training never changed any seed's answer.
  • The pre-registered convergence rule reports much later dates (185 and 101 million days) because of brief, harmless switches late in training. Section 7 explains this and proposes a labelled fix.
  • Exact recovery fails because exploration fades out before the top few portfolios are told apart. The best portfolio got 1,100 to 4,300 tries in the 20%-grid seeds that missed it; separating it from the runner-up takes about 1.1 million tries each.
  • No code was changed and nothing is committed. Rows 1 to 3 of the table in Section 7 touch only Albert's evaluation files and need a re-evaluation, not retraining.

What was run

Each experiment trains a stateless, tabular Q-learning agent. In every episode it picks one portfolio from the grid, the simulator draws 20 days of returns from the calibrated Gaussian model, and the reward is that portfolio's realized mean minus (γ/2) times its realized variance. The agent never sees μ, Σ or the benchmark portfolios. The two experiments differ only in the grid: 20% steps give 1,287 portfolios, 25% steps give 495. Both use the same master seed, so seed i sees the same simulated returns on both grids.

All settings come from src/config.py, fixed on 2026-10-16 before any full-scale run. The run took 01:35 to 05:00 EDT on 22 Oct 2026, about 300 seconds per seed.

20% vs. 25%: head to head

Why the agent misses the best portfolio

In plain terms: the rewards are very noisy compared with the differences the agent has to detect, and exploration fades before the top few portfolios have been tried often enough to tell them apart. Once ε is small, whichever near-top portfolio is ahead at that moment keeps almost all the episodes, and the others stop being tested.

The numbers behind that, for γ = 5 on the 20% grid:

    FIGURE 6.1

    How often the best portfolio was tried, in seeds that missed it

    Log scale. Each dot is a seed that ended on some other portfolio. The solid line is how often the final (chosen) portfolio was played. The dashed lines are roughly how many tries each of the top two portfolios needs before the gap between them is two standard errors: about 1.1 million on the 20% grid and 7.4 million on the 25% grid, where the top two are only 5.1 bps/yr apart.

    The same logic explains the late switches in Figures 4.4 and 5.4. A portfolio tried only a thousand or so times has a Q-value with a standard error of roughly 120 bps/yr, while the incumbent usually leads the near-top rivals by only 10 to 50 bps/yr. When exploration picks such a rival and the 20-day draw happens to be good, its Q-value can jump above the incumbent's. It then becomes greedy, gets played, and usually falls back within a handful of episodes (median 3 to 4). Those episodes restart the clock of the pre-registered convergence rule even though the agent's settled answer never changes.

    Findings as we would present them

    This section is written as the results part of the report would read if the project stopped here, organized by the assignment's Parts III to VI. Part I (data) and Part II (benchmark derivation) are covered by DATA_CLEANING.md and the benchmark module; their headline numbers are repeated where the analysis needs them.

    Answer to the central question

    Can a model-free learner recover a near-optimal portfolio from realized rewards alone? Mostly, but not exactly. With γ = 5 and 13 million 20-day episodes, every seed on both grids learns a portfolio built from SPY, GLD and IEF (plus at most one 20% or 25% slice of LQD or TIP), the same assets that make up the continuous optimum. The median learned portfolio gives up 19.8 bps/yr of certainty-equivalent return on the 20% grid and 14.3 bps/yr on the 25% grid, against a continuous optimum worth 5.31%/yr. That keeps about 96% to 97% of the attainable value. Equal weighting, for comparison, gives up about 310 bps/yr.

    Exact recovery of the best grid portfolio happens in 3 of 10 seeds on the 20% grid and 5 of 10 on the 25% grid. Statistical noise prevents it. One episode's reward has a standard deviation 7.4 times the best portfolio's expected reward, the best and second-best grid portfolios differ by 10.5 bps/yr, and the ε-greedy schedule stops sampling the runner-ups after a few thousand tries each, far short of the roughly 1.1 million needed to separate them.

    Part III: Q-learning design

    Part IV: Training and convergence

    Convergence was defined before any full-scale result existed: the final greedy action must hold for at least K = 1.3 million episodes (10% of training) through the end of training. Three secondary measures were also fixed in advance: the first time any action held for K episodes, the largest Q-value change staying below 1e-5 per 10,000-episode window, and the greedy portfolio staying within 25 bps/yr of the grid's best (an evaluation-side measure that uses the benchmark, so it plays no part in training). Every learned portfolio is scored on a separate evaluation sample of 10,000 fresh episodes drawn from its own random stream, never used to update Q-values; an independent rebuild of that sample matched to 1e-12.

    Convergence is faster and more frequent on the smaller action set: 10 of 10 seeds meet the primary rule on the 25% grid against 9 of 10 on the 20% grid, at a median of 101 against 185 million simulated days. The primary rule's dates are late because brief exploration-driven switches restart its clock. A post-hoc descriptive measure (final action greedy at every checkpoint from then on) puts the settling point at a median 18.5 and 47.1 million days, and in every seed the greedy action at the halfway checkpoint is already the final one. After the halfway point the only changes are brief excursions, none longer than 434 episodes.

    Statistical and economic convergence diverge. The Q-value tolerance is met by all 20 seeds, yet 3 seeds on the 20% grid and 1 on the 25% grid end more than 25 bps/yr from the best grid portfolio. Small Q-value changes show that the sample averages have stopped moving. Whether the right portfolio is on top depends on how many tries the top contenders received (Section 6).

    Part VI: Robustness

    Two checks were run with every other setting held fixed: the action-space resolution (25% vs. 20% grid, above) and risk aversion (γ = 3 and 8 against the γ = 5 baseline, all on the 20% grid). Because all experiments share the master seed, seed i sees the same returns and the same exploration draws across γ, so these are paired comparisons.

    The conclusions hold across γ: the agent always finds the right neighbourhood, exact recovery stays between 3 and 5 seeds in 10, and the median total gap stays between 11.9 and 19.8 bps/yr. By the median, γ = 5 has the largest learning gap, but γ = 3 has the heaviest tail: four of its seeds lose 60 to 81 bps/yr, and its mean learning gap is 30.6 bps/yr against 17.6 at γ = 5 and 13.0 at γ = 8. The number of close rivals does not explain this ranking on its own (three rivals within 25 bps/yr of the best at γ = 5, one at γ = 3 and one at γ = 8).

    Part VI: Research challenges

    Limitations and what is not done yet

    • Only γ = 5 has a second grid resolution; the 10% grid (43,758 portfolios) was not run. At the current budget each portfolio would get about 300 episodes if they were spread evenly, and about 32 random exploration tries under the current ε schedule, so it would need a different design to say anything.
    • A single calibration window and Gaussian returns. Alternative windows, shrinkage covariance and fat tails are listed in Part VI but not run.
    • The planned extensions (transaction costs with state, function approximation) are not started.
    • The pre-registered convergence rule is reported as fixed; the descriptive settling measure is post hoc and labelled that way.
