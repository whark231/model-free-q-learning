\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

We ask whether a model-free learner can recover a near-optimal
mean-variance portfolio from realized rewards alone. Nine ETFs are
calibrated on 5,152 daily excess returns (February 2006 to July 2026),
the estimated moments are frozen as the ground truth of a Gaussian
simulator, and a stateless tabular Q-learning agent chooses among the
1,287 long-only portfolios on a 20\% weight grid. At risk aversion γ = 5
the continuous long-only optimum is worth 530.5 bps/yr of
certainty-equivalent return. Across ten seeds the median gap is 14.1
bps/yr to the best portfolio on the grid and 19.8 bps/yr to the
continuous optimum, while an equal-weight portfolio is 310.9 bps/yr
behind. Three seeds of ten select the best grid portfolio exactly. Exact
recovery fails because the best and second-best grid portfolios differ
by 10.5 bps/yr while one 20-day episode's reward has a standard
deviation near 3,900 bps/yr. A finer grid lowers discretization error
and raises learning error, and at this training budget the total gap
does not improve. Two bonus extensions are reported with their failures.
Spline function approximation with a constant step size beats a
constant-step table on the 20\% grid at two of three risk aversions. On
the 10\% grid it loses to the \(1/N\) table at every risk aversion, in a
comparison that changes the step-size rule along with the
representation. With transaction costs and drifting holdings, the agent
learns to time its trades as costs rise. Its timing adds nothing
measurable at low costs, 0.8 bps/yr at \$1 billion and 6.2 at \$10
billion, most of the 7.8 the oracle gains from timing there. Its gap to
an oracle that knows the moments and costs narrows from 13.1 bps/yr at
zero cost to 2.8 at \$10 billion, mostly because its chosen target is
closer to the best one at that level, for reasons we have not
investigated. The four baseline experiments were specified before any
result existed; every other run is labeled post hoc.

\subsection{1. Introduction and central research
question}\label{introduction-and-central-research-question}

The assignment poses one question: ``Can a model-free learning algorithm
recover a near-optimal portfolio allocation when it observes realized
rewards but does not know expected returns or covariances?'' We answer
it in a controlled experiment. A mean vector μ and covariance matrix Σ
are estimated once from nine traded ETFs and then treated as the truth
of a synthetic market. A mean-variance investor who knows μ and Σ can
solve for the optimal portfolio directly. Our agent never sees them. It
picks a portfolio, holds it for a short episode of simulated returns,
receives the realized mean-variance reward, and updates a table with one
value per portfolio.

Three portfolios and two gaps organize the report. The continuous
optimum \(w^*\) is the best long-only, fully invested portfolio with any
weights. The best grid portfolio is the best one the agent is allowed to
choose. The learned portfolio is what the agent chose. The
discretization gap is the loss of the best grid portfolio against
\(w^*\), and the learning gap is the loss of the learned portfolio
against the best grid portfolio. Their sum is the total gap. Every gap
is a certainty-equivalent loss, \(252 \times 10^4 \times \Delta U\) in
basis points per year, where \(U\) is daily mean-variance utility. It
reads as the sure annual return the investor gives up.

Four distinctions run through the report: the continuous optimum and the
best grid portfolio (Section 3); learning error and discretization error
(Section 5); calibration, which uses historical data once, and
evaluation, which uses simulated draws that never updated a Q-value
(Section 2); and statistical convergence (the agent's own table stops
changing) and economic convergence (the chosen portfolio stays within 25
bps/yr of the best available), reported side by side in Section 5.

The answer, at γ = 5 on the 20\% grid, is a median gap across ten seeds
of 14.1 bps/yr to the best attainable portfolio and 19.8 bps/yr to the
continuous optimum, out of 530.5 bps/yr available, where equal weighting
loses 310.9. Three seeds of ten recover the best grid portfolio exactly.
The others stop on a close neighbor because the differences among the
top portfolios are about 370 times smaller than the noise in a single
reward.

Only the four baseline experiments (γ = 5 on the 20\% and 25\% grids, γ
= 3 and γ = 8 on the 20\% grid) had their settings and convergence rules
fixed before any result existed. The settings were committed on 16
October 2026 and the first full run finished on 22 October. All other
runs were designed after seeing the baseline and are labeled post hoc
wherever they appear.

\subsection{2. ETF data, sample construction, and
calibration}\label{etf-data-sample-construction-and-calibration}

\textbf{Universe and sources.} The nine ETFs are SPY, EFA and EEM
(equity), IEF, LQD and TIP (fixed income), and GLD, VNQ and DBC (real
assets). Daily prices come from Yahoo Finance through \texttt{yfinance}
1.7.0, downloaded on 24 September 2026. The daily risk-free rate is the
Kenneth French Data Library daily factor file. The environment is
defined on excess returns.

\textbf{Returns and adjustment.} Returns are simple returns on the
Adjusted Close, \(r_t = P_t / P_{t-1} - 1\). No log returns are used
anywhere in the project. We verified the adjustment convention in two
ways: the Adjusted Close and Close columns differ only on distribution
dates, by the amount the distribution implies (worst discrepancy
\(6.0 \times 10^{-4}\)), and the EEM three-for-one split of 24 July 2008
leaves no jump in the return series.

\textbf{Sample.} The calibration sample runs from 7 February 2006 to 31
July 2026 and has 5,152 daily observations. The start is set by DBC,
which began trading on 6 February 2006. The end is set by the risk-free
file, which stops on 31 July 2026; we truncated the sample there in
preference to forward-filling the rate. Dates are aligned by an inner
join across the nine ETFs. The 5,214 raw rows for SPY reduce to 5,152
through five documented drops (1 incomplete final session, 23 dates
before DBC's inception, 1 date missing at the vendor, 1 row lost to
differencing, 36 dates after the risk-free file ends). Extreme days are
kept, including EEM's +22.77\% on 13 October 2008.

\textbf{One frequency.} The mean vector, the covariance matrix, risk
aversion and all rewards are daily. Annualization (×252 for means and
utilities, ×√252 for volatilities) is applied only when reporting.

\textbf{Ground-truth environment.} The simulator draws
\(r_{t+1} = \mu + L z_{t+1}\) with \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_9)\) and
\(L\) the Cholesky factor of Σ, so that
\(r \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\) independently across days. The
moments are fixed for every training and evaluation draw. They are the
truth inside the experiment and are not a claim about future markets.
Training returns, the agent's exploration draws and evaluation returns
come from separate random streams derived from one master seed
(20261016), so evaluation data never overlaps training data and seed
\(i\) sees the same simulated returns in every comparable run.

\textbf{Leakage guard.} The agent's code cannot import the data loader,
the benchmark module or the data files; unit tests enforce an import
allowlist. The agent receives its own action index and a scalar reward,
and nothing else.

Two properties of the data bear on later sections. Excess kurtosis is
large for most of the ETFs (58.9 for LQD, 17.8 for EEM, 17.2 for VNQ,
15.0 for SPY), while the simulator is Gaussian, so the simulated world
has thinner tails than the data it was calibrated on (Section 6.5).

\subsection{3. Theoretical benchmark and feasible portfolio
set}\label{theoretical-benchmark-and-feasible-portfolio-set}

\textbf{Objective.} The investor maximizes daily mean-variance utility

\[U(w) = w^\top \mu - \frac{\gamma}{2}\, w^\top \Sigma\, w .\]

The baseline risk aversion is γ = 5, with γ = 3 and γ = 8 as robustness
values. We chose γ = 5 on 16 October 2026, before any run. It lies
inside the range of 2 to 7 that the meta-analysis of Elminejad, Havranek
and Irsova (2025) reports for the finance literature, consistent with
the household survey of Barsky et al.~(1997), in which most respondents
fall in the least risk-tolerant category, and well under the upper bound
of 10 that Mehra and Prescott (1985) regard as plausible. A value
implied by the dataset itself (2.9) was considered and rejected so that
the data would not select its own risk aversion.

\textbf{Budget-only closed form.} With only the constraint
\(\mathbf{1}^\top w = 1\), the Lagrangian is
\(\mathcal{L} = w^\top\mu - \tfrac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w - \eta(\mathbf{1}^\top w - 1)\).
The first-order condition
\(\mu - \gamma \Sigma w - \eta \mathbf{1} = 0\) gives
\(w = \tfrac{1}{\gamma}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - \eta \mathbf{1})\), and
substituting into the budget constraint gives the multiplier:

\[w_{\text{budget}} = \frac{1}{\gamma}\,\Sigma^{-1}\left(\mu - \eta\,\mathbf{1}\right), \qquad \eta = \frac{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mu - \gamma}{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}} .\]

At γ = 5 this portfolio holds 182\% in SPY and −88\% in EFA (Appendix B
gives the script). It agrees with an independent numerical solve to
within \(1.5 \times 10^{-6}\) and violates the long-only constraint, so
it is not the benchmark for the learning comparison.

\textbf{Feasible set and the two benchmarks.} The agent's feasible set
is long-only and fully invested: \(w_i \ge 0\) and \(\sum_i w_i = 1\).
The theoretical benchmark is solved numerically under exactly these
constraints, so theory and learning face identical restrictions. For
every specification we report two benchmarks. The continuous optimum
\(w^*\) maximizes \(U\) over the whole simplex. The best grid portfolio
maximizes \(U\) over the agent's finite action set and is found by
evaluating every action.

\textbf{Action set.} An action is a vector of nine weights in multiples
of a step \(h\) that sum to one. The set is generated from the number of
assets and the step alone. No moment enters its construction and the
optimum is not inserted. The set has 495 portfolios at \(h\) = 25\%,
1,287 at 20\% (the baseline), 43,758 at 10\%, and 3,108,105 at 5\%,
which we count and do not train.

\subsection{4. Q-learning design and
implementation}\label{q-learning-design-and-implementation}

\textbf{State.} The baseline is stateless. Returns are independent and
identically distributed, so nothing observable at the decision date
predicts the next episode.

\textbf{Action.} Each episode the agent selects one of the 1,287 grid
portfolios and holds its weights for \(B\) days.

\textbf{Reward.} The reward is the episodic realized mean-variance
reward the assignment recommends. For the chosen portfolio's daily
excess returns \(p_1, \dots, p_B\),

\[R = \bar{p} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\, s_p^2, \qquad s_p^2 = \frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{t=1}^{B} (p_t - \bar{p})^2 .\]

Because the sample mean and the sample variance with divisor \(B-1\) are
unbiased, \(E[R \mid w] = U(w)\) exactly. The reward and the theoretical
objective therefore agree in expectation, and the benchmark needs no
adjustment.

\textbf{Update.} The standard Q-learning update is
\(Q(s_t,a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t) + \alpha\left[R_{t+1} + \rho \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1},a') - Q(s_t,a_t)\right]\).
With no state and discount factor ρ = 0 it becomes

\[Q(a_t) \leftarrow Q(a_t) + \alpha_t \left[ R_{t+1} - Q(a_t) \right].\]

The assignment describes ρ = 0 as ``a clean bandit-like benchmark''. A
positive ρ would add the same continuation value to every action's
target and would not change their ranking.

\textbf{Exploration.} Actions are ε-greedy with
\(\varepsilon_t = e^{-\beta t}\), where \(t\) counts episodes. With
probability \(\varepsilon_t\) the agent draws a portfolio uniformly at
random; otherwise it takes the portfolio with the highest Q-value.

\subsection{5. Convergence and benchmark
comparison}\label{convergence-and-benchmark-comparison}

This section reports the four pre-registered experiments, ten seeds
each.

\subsubsection{5.1 Convergence criteria, defined before the
results}\label{convergence-criteria-defined-before-the-results}

The primary rule is statistical and uses only the agent's own table. A
seed has converged when its greedy portfolio stops changing and then
stays unchanged until the end of training, for at least \(K\) = 1.3
million episodes (10\% of the budget). Three further rules were
pre-registered. Two are statistical: the greedy portfolio is unchanged
for any window of \(K\) episodes, and the largest change in any Q-value
stays below \(10^{-5}\) in every later 10,000-episode window. The third
is economic and uses the benchmark for evaluation only: the greedy
portfolio stays within 25 bps/yr of the best grid portfolio from some
checkpoint onward. The 25 bps/yr line is 4.8\% of the best grid
portfolio's value at γ = 5 and contains 4 of the 1,287 portfolios, the
best one included.

One descriptive measure, ``settled'', was added after the results were
known and is labeled post hoc. It records the first time the greedy
portfolio equals the seed's final portfolio and thereafter leaves it
only for brief excursions.

\subsubsection{5.6 What prevented exact
recovery}\label{what-prevented-exact-recovery}

The agent's problem is to rank portfolios whose expected rewards are
nearly equal using very noisy samples. At γ = 5 the second-best grid
portfolio (TIP in place of IEF) is 10.5 bps/yr behind the best, and four
portfolios, the best included, lie within 25 bps/yr of the best. One
episode's reward has a standard deviation near 3,900 bps/yr. Separating
the top two at two standard errors of the difference in their sample
means requires about 1.1 million evaluations of each. During exploration
each portfolio receives about 1,097 random visits, three orders of
magnitude fewer.

In the seven seeds that missed the best portfolio on the 20\% grid, it
was tried between 1,118 and 4,282 times (Figure A8). Once ε is small the
agent revisits only its current favorite, so a good portfolio whose
early sample mean was unlucky is rarely tried again. A post hoc
diagnostic added on 4 November is consistent with this account. Across
all 1,287 portfolios, the learned Q-values order portfolios much like
true utility does (median rank correlation 0.821). At the top they do
not: only about a third of the ten truly best portfolios appear in the
agent's own top ten (median 3.5 of 10 across seeds).

\subsubsection{6.3 Extension: function approximation (post
hoc)}\label{extension-function-approximation-post-hoc}

The table treats 1,287 portfolios as unrelated, although portfolios with
similar weights have similar utility. Function approximation replaces
the table with \(\hat{Q}(a) = \theta^\top \phi(w_a)\) and updates θ by
stochastic gradient steps on the same rewards. We test two feature maps.
Quadratic features (45 columns) match the quadratic form of \(U\) and
serve as a correctly specified reference. Additive and pairwise cubic
B-splines (909 columns, rank 369 on the 20\% grid) assume only
smoothness and are the model of interest. The \(1/N(a)\) rule has no
counterpart for shared parameters, so these runs use a constant step
size, tuned on a separate master seed (20261103) at γ = 5 only. Each
step size was chosen by training the tuning seeds in full and scoring
their final greedy portfolios on 200,000 simulated evaluation episodes.
This is validation through the simulator, which an investor with one
history of returns could not do. No closed-form benchmark entered the
choice. The like-for-like tabular comparison is therefore a table with a
constant step size (\texttt{tabular-const}, α = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴), and the
baseline is shown for reference. The runs were registered on 5 and 11
November before training.

extension, since the table cannot visit 43,758 portfolios often enough.
The spline agent's mean gap is 78.8, 140.6 and 59.6 bps/yr against the
table's 11.7, 22.0 and 5.8, with paired differences of +67.1, +118.6 and
+53.8 (standard errors 9.1, 12.2 and 9.8), and it is worse on 10, 10 and

was run on the 10\% grid, so the two agents differ in step-size rule as
well as in representation, and on the 20\% grid the constant step alone
costs the table 25.9, 76.6 and 30.2 bps/yr. Raising exploration to 10\%
at halfway does not help (paired changes of +2.6, +15.0 and +0.6, each
within about one standard error) and costs 25 to 28 bps/yr per episode
in training regret. No seed of the sixty meets the primary convergence

The spline model ranks the whole grid far better than the table does
(median Spearman correlation 0.976 against 0.543 at γ = 5) and places
none of the true top ten in its own top ten at the median (Table A1,

mean-field calculation made before the run had predicted gaps of about
33.6, 0 and 1.3 bps/yr; the realized gaps are 78.8, 140.6 and 59.6.

so their comparison mixes representation and step size, and on the 10\%
grid the step (2.778 × 10⁻⁵) was matched to the noise level without
re-tuning. We report the 10\% grid outcome as a negative result for this
configuration.

\subsubsection{6.4 Extension: transaction costs and rebalancing (post
hoc)}\label{extension-transaction-costs-and-rebalancing-post-hoc}

\textbf{A first design that failed.} Our first design kept the nine
assets and the 20\% grid, made the state the previously chosen portfolio
and charged a quadratic cost for moving, which gives 1,287 × 1,287 ≈
1.66 million (holding, target) pairs and, with no drift in holdings, no
reason to rebalance. At zero cost the median learned policy is worth
56.6 bps/yr against the grid oracle's 524.8, which matches the average
over all 1,287 grid portfolios (56.7), so the agent learned nothing and
we redesigned the problem (Table A2, Figure A13).

\textbf{The redesign.} The universe is the three ETFs in the support of
the nine-asset optimum (SPY, IEF, GLD), with γ = 5. Time is divided into
blocks of 63 trading days. Within a block the number of shares is fixed,
so the weights drift with realized returns, and the drifted weights are
the next block's state (Figure A12). At the start of each block the
agent either holds or trades to one of 1,326 targets on a 2\% grid. A
trade from holdings \(s\) to target \(x\) costs a fraction of wealth

\[c(s, x) = \kappa \lVert x - s \rVert_1 + \frac{\lambda}{2} \lVert x - s \rVert_2^2 ,\]

a proportional term for the bid-ask spread and a quadratic term for
price impact, in the spirit of Gârleanu and Pedersen (2013). The block
reward is the realized mean-variance reward of Section 4 computed on the
drifting portfolio, minus the cost divided by 63, so it stays in daily
units. The agent maximizes the discounted sum of block rewards with a
discount factor of 0.95 per block (about 0.81 per year). The discount
now affects the policy, because a trade in one block determines the next
block's holdings and costs.

The cost parameters come from the ETFs' own quoted spreads and trading
volume under a square-root impact model, at three fund sizes: \$100
million (κ = 1.63 bp, λ = 4.4 × 10⁻³), \$1 billion (κ = 3.90 bp, λ =
1.43 × 10⁻²) and \$10 billion (κ = 11.04 bp, λ = 4.57 × 10⁻²), plus a
zero-cost control.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} The oracle solves the same discounted problem by
value iteration on a 1\% lattice with full knowledge of μ, Σ, κ and λ.
Three simpler reference policies are scored on the same simulated paths:
always rebalance to the best no-cost target (0.46, 0.14, 0.40); trade to
the best target in the first block and never rebalance again; and a
myopic policy that optimizes one block at a time. All policies are
scored by expected discounted utility over 4,000 shared starting points
and paths of 90 blocks, in bps/yr.

On the evaluation paths the oracle beats always rebalancing by 0.04,
0.42, 1.78 and 7.79 bps/yr at zero cost, \$100 million, \$1 billion and
\$10 billion (standard errors 0.04, 0.22, 0.36 and 0.64), so the gain
available from timing trades is small. The lead computed inside the
oracle's own model (0.01, 0.21, 1.32 and 6.94) agrees within sampling
error.

\textbf{Agent.} A table over continuous holdings is not possible, so the
agent uses a linear Q-function with 87 spline features of the post-trade
weights and two cost features, the L1 and squared L2 size of the trade,
whose coefficients it learns from net rewards. It never sees μ, Σ, κ, λ
or the oracle. Each seed trains for 4 million blocks (252 million
simulated days). The step size (3 × 10⁻⁴) was tuned on a separate seed
(20261112) at the \$1 billion level, and the reported policy averages
the parameters over the final 10\% of training. Twelve expectations were
registered on 20 November before any final run (Table A3).

18\% to 32\% in IEF against 14\% in the best target (Figure A15). At

from μ and Σ, while the agent has to find its target from rewards.

At \$10 billion the agent's timing is worth +6.2 bps/yr relative to

only 1.1 against rebalancing to the best target, and the agent beats

holds in 17\% of blocks against the oracle's 45\%, and its turnover
(2.11\% per block) lies between the oracle's (1.55\%) and always
rebalancing's (2.98\%). Its timing captures 6.2 of the 7.8 bps/yr the
oracle gains over always rebalancing. Most of the narrowing of the gap
to the oracle comes from its target, though: rebalancing to its own
target loses 1.1 bps/yr at \$10 billion against 9 to 13 at the lower
levels, and why its target is so much better at \$10 billion has not
been investigated.

The agent learns the quadratic cost coefficient and does not learn the
linear one (Figure A17). The quadratic coefficient is within a factor of
two of the truth on 9 of 10 seeds at \$100 million and on all ten at \$1
billion and \$10 billion. The linear coefficient is zero or below 0.2 of
the truth on all ten seeds at \$10 billion and at or below 0.15 on six
seeds at \$1 billion, and it is 2.8 to 14.9 times too high on five seeds
at \$100 million.

Of the twelve registered expectations, eight were met and four partly
met (Table A3). The first was missed at the three lower levels, where we
predicted a gap to the oracle of 1 to 8 bps/yr and observed 13.1, 13.1
and 10.0. The pilot on three tuning seeds had understated the favorite
target's static utility loss, which was 2 to 6 bps/yr there and is 9.75
on the final ten seeds at \$1 billion.

\textbf{Disclosures.} The forty runs are post hoc relative to the
baseline. Benchmark information entered several design decisions, none
of which reaches the agent during training: the three-ETF universe is
the support of the nine-asset optimum, the cost levels were chosen with
the oracle's results in view, the two cost features build in the
functional form of the cost, and the convergence rule was revised three
times before it was frozen on 20 November. The registered expectations
lean on a \$1 billion tuning pilot of the same agent, benchmark
diagnostics included. Our precision target was missed at \$1 billion.
The full list is in Appendix C.

\subsubsection{6.5 Research challenges and
limitations}\label{research-challenges-and-limitations}

\textbf{Estimation error in expected returns and covariances.} μ and Σ
are estimated once from 5,152 days and then treated as known. The
standard error of each annualized mean is 1.4 to 6.4 percentage points
(Table 1). SPY's mean of 10.7\% has a standard error of 4.27, which
exceeds the difference in mean between about half of the asset pairs (17
of 36). The optimum's concentration in SPY and GLD therefore rests on
mean estimates the data cannot distinguish from several alternatives.
Our results are conditional on the calibrated moments and are silent on
whether that optimum would be a good portfolio out of sample. We did not
run an alternative calibration window or a shrinkage estimate of Σ.

\textbf{Conditioning and numerical stability.} When some assets move
almost in lockstep, the data can barely tell them apart, and any formula
that relies on telling them apart can turn small estimation errors into
large, offsetting bets. Our covariance matrix is well enough behaved for
the simulator to generate returns reliably (its condition number, a
standard measure of this problem, is 268). The weak spot is the three
equity funds, which move closely together. The formula that allows short
sales reacts to them by putting 182\% of wealth in SPY and −88\% in EFA
at γ = 5, and by offsetting IEF (+27\%) against TIP (−27\%)
(\texttt{results/benchmarks/closed\_form\_budget\_only.csv}). The
long-only optimum the agent is measured against cannot take offsetting
positions like these, holds only three ETFs, and is much less exposed.

\textbf{The curse of dimensionality.} The number of actions grows from
495 to 3,108,105 as the step falls from 25\% to 5\%. At a fixed budget,
random visits per portfolio fall from about 1,097 on the 20\% grid to 85
on the 10\% grid even after doubling the episodes, and no seed of thirty
converges there. On that grid, spline function approximation with a
constant step did worse than the table (mean gap 78.8 against 11.7
bps/yr at γ = 5; Section 6.3), and the first transaction-cost design,
with 1.66 million state-action pairs, learned nothing (Section 6.4). In
each case the limit is set by reward noise relative to the utility
differences that matter, together with the number of alternatives.

\textbf{Discretization error and benchmark leakage.} The discretization
gap (11.74, 5.74 and 1.29 bps/yr at γ = 5 on the three grids) is
reported separately from the learning gap everywhere, the action set is
built from the asset count and the step only with tests that stop agent
code importing benchmark code, and every case of benchmark information
reaching a human design decision is disclosed (the episode budget in
Section 4 and the transaction-cost design in Section 6.4).

\textbf{Consistency between reward and objective.} With the \(B-1\)
divisor, \(E[R \mid w] = U(w)\) exactly, a property the unit tests
verify by simulation, and the headline gaps are computed from the exact
moments. In the transaction-cost extension the block reward is net of
cost and computed on drifting weights, so its expectation is not exactly
\(U\) of the starting weights; the oracle maximizes the same discounted
objective and accounts for the drift by simulation.

\textbf{The limits of a stationary simulated environment.} Returns are
independent Gaussian draws with fixed moments. There are no regimes, no
predictability and no fat tails, although the data has excess kurtosis
as high as 58.9. The baseline uses 260 million simulated days per seed,
about a million years of daily data, to choose among portfolios that an
investor with twenty years of data could not tell apart. Sample-average
learning works here because the moments never change; with drifting
moments the \(1/N\) rule would average over regimes and stop adapting.
The experiment shows that the learning problem is solvable in principle
at a large cost in data, and it leaves open whether the method is
practical on historical returns.

\subsection{7. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

\textbf{What the agent learned.} From rewards alone, with no knowledge
of expected returns or covariances, the Q-learning agent learned to hold
U.S. equities and gold on every seed and to add a bond fund on most.
That is the composition of the mean-variance optimum. It also followed
the optimum toward bonds as risk aversion rose.

\textbf{How close it came.} At γ = 5 on the 20\% grid the median gap
across seeds is 14.1 bps/yr to the best attainable portfolio and 19.8
bps/yr to the continuous optimum, out of 530.5 available, where equal
weighting loses 310.9. Three seeds of ten recover the best grid
portfolio exactly at γ = 5, and five of ten at γ = 3 and γ = 8. Nine of
ten seeds meet the convergence rule set in advance.

\textbf{What prevented exact recovery.} The top grid portfolios are
about 10 bps/yr apart and a single episode's reward has a standard
deviation near 3,900 bps/yr. Ranking them reliably would take about a
million evaluations of each, and exploration supplies about a thousand.
The agent finds the right region of the simplex and stops on one of
several near-equivalent portfolios inside it.

\textbf{The trade-off in action-space design.} A finer grid removes
discretization error and adds learning error. At this training budget
the two roughly cancel between the 20\% and 10\% grids at γ = 5, the
10\% grid is worse at γ = 3 and γ = 8, and the coarser 25\% grid has the
smaller median total gap.

\textbf{The extensions.} Both are post hoc and both include a negative
result. Spline function approximation with a constant step ranks a large
action set well overall and picks badly at the top. On the 10\% grid it
loses to the \(1/N\) table at every risk aversion, in a comparison that
also changes the step-size rule. With transaction costs, a first design
learned nothing. The redesigned agent, which never sees μ, Σ or the cost
parameters, gets closer to the full-information oracle as costs rise
(13.1 bps/yr behind at zero cost, 2.8 at \$10 billion). At \$10 billion
it beats always rebalancing on all ten seeds and is statistically
indistinguishable from the myopic policy, which knows μ, Σ and the costs
(−0.5 bps/yr, SE 0.54, ahead on 5 of 10 seeds). Its trade timing
captures 6.2 of the oracle's 7.8 bps/yr timing gain at \$10 billion, but
most of the narrowing comes from a better target at that level, which we
have not explained. It learns the quadratic cost, and at the lower
levels its choice of target costs 9 to 13 bps/yr.

\textbf{Limits.} These results hold in a stationary Gaussian world whose
moments are taken as true, with a data budget no investor has. The
pre-registered experiments (the 20\% and 25\% grids at γ = 5, and γ = 3
and 8) carry the headline; the post hoc runs support it and do not
replace it.

\subsection{Appendix C. Disclosures for the transaction-cost
extension}\label{appendix-c.-disclosures-for-the-transaction-cost-extension}

The forty runs are post hoc relative to the baseline and came from
commit \texttt{1787f0d} with a clean working tree. Benchmark information
entered the design in several ways, none of which reaches the agent
during training.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  The three-ETF universe is the support of the nine-asset optimum.
\item
  The cost levels were chosen with the oracle's results in view,
  although κ and λ themselves come from our liquidity data.
\item
  The two cost features build in the functional form of the cost; the
  agent learns two coefficients, constrained to be nonnegative.
\item
  The learning rule, the parameter averaging and the convergence rule
  were chosen with pilot diagnostics that used the benchmark; the
  convergence rule changed three times before it was frozen on 20
  November, and under the earlier versions applied to the last iterate
  no seed converges at any level.
\item
  The convergence check reads the policy at checkpoints, so a switch
  that reverts between checkpoints goes unseen.
\item
  Tuning used the \$1 billion level only, and a first set of six pilot
  runs was discarded.
\item
  The always-rebalance and trade-once references use the best no-cost
  target, computed from μ and Σ.
\item
  One cost applies to every asset pair, which undercharges the typical
  SPY-against-GLD trade by about a fifth.
\item
  The oracle summary and the tuning records were produced from code with
  uncommitted changes; the final agent runs were not.
\item
  Our precision target at \$1 billion was missed: the paired standard
  error of one seed's gap is 0.94 bps/yr against a target of 0.59.
\item
  Drift is applied to excess returns, with an effect bounded at 7.1 ×
  10⁻⁶.
\item
  One of the oracle's verification checks was restated after the fact,
  and another is a two-standard-error bound.
\item
  The twelve registered expectations (Table A3) lean on the \$1 billion
  tuning pilot, three runs of the same agent on a different master seed,
  and on that pilot's benchmark diagnostics (oracle gaps, the favorite
  target's utility loss, the true cost coefficients). They are informed
  by benchmark information about the same agent on other seeds, and were
  frozen on 20 November before any final run.
\end{enumerate}
